Streaming registration

Rigid registration of whole-slide histology at its native resolution, in bounded memory.

Scroll

A terabyte per section.

Whole-slide sections are scanned at 0.5 µm per pixel. At that resolution, in-memory registration tools need roughly a terabyte per section, so analyses have usually dropped to half resolution.

Write the output tile by tile.

Each output tile maps its four corners back into the source and reads only that footprint, through a bounded cache.

Skip what isn't tissue.

A tile whose footprint misses the tissue is written as a constant background, without reading anything at all.

Straight into a pyramid.

Finished tiles are encoded and appended to a tiled pyramidal file, level after level. TIFF, OME-TIFF and OME-Zarr all work.

What it does to the numbers

600 GB
Of registered output, produced in about 12 GB of memory.
< 4 min
On 32 cores, down from about half an hour on two. Past that, the output writer is the bottleneck.
Exact
At level 1, the output matches the in-memory pipeline exactly.

For each level of the output pyramid, for each output tile

  1. Inverse-map the tile's four corners into the source
  2. Does the footprint meet tissue? If not, emit a constant background tile
  3. Read the source tiles through the cache
  4. Warp the tile with a bilinear kernel
  5. Encode the tile and append it to the pyramid

The section in the animation is a sagittal Nissl section of a 24-week fetal brain from DHARANI (FB85); its misalignment is simulated to show the method.

Streaming out-of-core rigid registration of whole-slide histology at native resolution

Chrisline Sam Christopher, Supriti Mulay, Richa Verma, Keerthi Ram, Sivathanu Neelakantan, Mohanasankar Sivaprakasam

  • Rigidly aligns whole-slide sections at the scanner's full 0.5 µm per pixel, where in-memory tools would need about a terabyte per section.
  • Works in output tiles: it reads only the source region each tile needs, skips tiles outside the tissue, and keeps a bounded cache.
  • About 600 GB of output from about 12 GB of memory; at level 1 the output matches the in-memory pipeline exactly.
  • From about half an hour on 2 cores to under four minutes on 32, after which writing the output is the bottleneck.
